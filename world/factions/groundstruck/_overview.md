# The Groundstruck — Goliaths of Lone Peak

> **Lone Peak** is the common name — the obvious one, given by everyone who has ever looked
> at it from the road. People also call it the Lonely Mountain, or just the Peak. It is not
> an official name; no one official has ever been up there. The goliaths call it
> **Ulearos** (oo-leh-AR-ohs).

## What We Know

- **The mountain stands alone**, just off the borders of [[world/factions/araethilion/_overview|Araethilion]].
  It is one of the knife-edged peaks — the heights Araethilion's elves explicitly do *not* claim.
- **It was a flying city.** It fell, crashed into the land, and became a mountain.
- **It fell in year 0** — the fall of [[calendar-reckoning-of-the-fall|Ao Sathanum]], the
  cataclysm the whole world dates its calendar from. The mountain is a piece of that
  ancient world, and like the rest of it, poorly understood.
- **It stands alone** between Araethilion, Ardenhaven and the Garrok Confederation, north of
  the great lake — visible from the road and from all three lands. Huge.
- **It cannot be reached without flight or real climbing gear.** No road, no pass, no stair.
- **The goliaths live on the peak and in the ruins below it.**
- **Sabriel saw it twice** (see her timeline):
  - **2346** — an entire city tipped at a forty-five-degree lean, streets and towers
    half-collapsed, "as though the mountain itself had rolled onto its side."
  - **2552** — "time had erased the last traces of that fallen city, the mountain made
    whole again." From below, the ruins are gone. *What actually happened to them is a
    question for this faction.*

## Belief System

> Built as a tree, one question at a time — each answer decides whether we dig deeper or
> branch somewhere new. Answered branches first; open questions below.

### The City (settled)
Ao Sathanum's flying city, fallen in year 0. Its purpose and its people belong to world
lore and are not well understood — not by the goliaths, not by anyone.

### The First Legend — How the Goliaths Came to Be
As the legend has it, when the peak fell, it scraped the lowest stars from the sky — and
those stars became the goliaths. It happened so long ago that the truth is lost; what
remains is the story. Perhaps it is true in the sense that a god did a thing. Perhaps it is
all made up.

What is *not* untrue: **the goliaths are star druids.** Whatever their origin, the sky
answers them.

The legend is told the way a people explain how a thing came into the world — extremely
poetic, handed down by voice, more true than accurate.

#### Gifted, Mostly — Sky or Earth?
By default the goliaths see their fall as a **gift**: they were given land high above
everything, safe from all.

But when they are annoyed — especially with lowlanders — they say:

> *"Let me be among the stars again."*

It doesn't sound like an insult, but it is one. It means *I am sick of you, and I wish I
were taken back up to the high sky.* It carries a second sense too: *I want to go home* —
because the summit of their mountain, so close to the stars, is the nearest thing to the
sky they came from.

### The World Below
As a society, the goliaths **avoid the lowlands** and stay out of world politics entirely.
Lone Peak takes no side, signs no treaty, and answers no envoy.

As individuals, they are **free to do as they please.** Now and then one ventures down into
the world, and now and then someone from the world ventures up the mountain. The people do
not get involved; the person may.

#### Going Down
A goliath who leaves the summit is **both admired and worried over** — the way a family
feels about a loved one going abroad. Everyone knows the world below is dangerous, and that
is exactly why they admire those who leave.

There is no single correct feeling about it. Each goliath feels differently; it is a
complex society, and a departure will be met with pride, fear, envy and grief in the same
room.

### The Star-Druid Path
Star druidry is **trained, not born.** Anyone can learn it — even a human who reaches the
summit can train in their ways.

Among the goliaths it is a **strong expectation**, close to universal, with few
exceptions:

- **Those who hold power are star druids.**
- **A goliath who does not become a druid cannot leave the mountain at all.** They are
  bound to the city.

So it is strongly encouraged — not by birthright, but by everything that matters in their
society depending on it.

#### Why the Unshifted Stay
Nothing forbids a non-druid from leaving. **It is a physical difficulty, not a law.** With
rope and real climbing skill, a goliath can get down — but there is no easy way back up.

Learning to shift into a bird (or anything winged) is what opens the mountain. It means
flying from place to place — between buildings, across their own city — never mind having
the option to leave at all. A goliath who never learns is grounded in a city built for
wings.

### The City on the Peak
The goliaths live **in and on** the mountain — never under it, and never low on it.

- **The old buildings** — Ao Sathanum's ruins, driven into the rock by the fall — are what
  they live *in*.
- **On top of them**, they build their own.
- **Everything they live in is accessible only from high up.** Not tunnels under the peak,
  not settlements on the lower slopes: the high ruins and the summit, reachable by wings or
  a very long climb.

> **Reconciling Sabriel (proposed, not confirmed):** what she sketched in 2346 was the ruin
> she could see from below, on the lower flanks. By 2552 those low ruins had been reclaimed
> by rock and time — "the mountain made whole again." The goliaths' city was always higher
> than she could see.

### Leadership
**One leader, chosen.** When a leader passes, the druids choose the next — much the way a
pope is chosen.

- **The position is spiritual**, but because the entire people is deeply spiritual, the
  spiritual head carries **the most weight on every decision.** Pope-like authority.
- **Influence belongs to the druids.** The non-druid few have little voice.
- **It runs democratically** — the leader leads, but decisions move through the people.

**Population: about 1,000.** More than Elderholt's five hundred, far smaller than any other
faction. Small for a society — small enough that everyone knows the leader by face.

#### The New Star — Choosing the Leader
The leader is **chosen by the sky.** The goliaths believe the next leader is born on the
day a new star appears.

1. **A leader dies.**
2. **The people wait** for a new star to appear in the sky.
3. **On that day, they go searching** for the child born under it.

Because the population is so small, there is usually **only one newborn** on the day of
the new star, and the choice is obvious. If there is more than one, there can still only
be one: the children are raised and watched, and the people look for the signs and traits
that mark the true leader.

- **To date, the chosen has always been one of their own.** Nothing prevents the star from
  choosing a child of another race — it simply has never happened.
- **Mistakes are possible.** When two children share the day, the people can choose wrong.
- **It is not just religion. It is real.** A star genuinely appears, and the chosen child
  is unnaturally gifted: they learn druidry with uncanny speed, and their connection to
  the stars and to druidic magic runs deeper than anyone else's.

> **Hook:** the star has never chosen an outsider. Nothing says it can't — or that the
> child has to be born on the mountain.

#### The Years Between
Between a leader's death and the day the star's child is grown, **no one holds the
seat.** The druids' natural leaders bicker among themselves, and direction goes to whoever
has the most influence and makes the most sense at the moment — like a flock of birds
negotiating where the nearest water is. Leadership shifts from voice to voice until the
chosen is ready.

### Souls and Stars — Death
**Every goliath has a star**, linked to them for life.

- **The leader is a new star** — a new soul, a new spirit, not a rebirth. That is why the
  goliaths believe a new soul is best to lead: it sees with fresh eyes.
- **Every other newborn is an existing star** — a soul that already was. Perhaps reborn;
  perhaps never born into this world before, but pre-existing all the same.

**Death is not death.** It is the soul becoming unattached from the mortal coil — a
severing of its connection to the world and to its body. The star was always in the sky;
the goliath was always, in some sense, already there.

Their words for it:

> *"Let me know the sky once again."*

They were in the sky. They are in the sky still. Dying is simply remembering it.

> **Two sayings, not one.** *"Let me know the sky once again"* is sacred — about death and
> remembering. *"Let me be among the stars again"* is the polite insult — *I am sick of
> you.* Outsiders rarely notice the difference. To them, goliaths are always going on about
> the sky — which is exactly what makes the insult work: a goliath can say it to a
> lowlander's face, and the lowlander hears piety.

#### Mourning
The grief is real but particular: the same mourning you feel when someone moves to another
country for the rest of their life. You know you will never see them again — until, in
this case, you die too.

#### The Body
The body is only a container. The goliaths **burn their dead** so that nothing is left to
capture or contain the soul — to be sure all of it is released, and nothing remains to hold
the spirit down.

#### Finding Your Star
No goliath is born knowing which star is theirs. **It is a discovery.** The more a goliath
learns who they are, the more they instinctively know which star in the sky belongs to
them.

- **At death**, it is said, a goliath locks their gaze on their star in their final
  moments.
- **Not every goliath finds their star** in one lifetime. One who never finds it, it is
  said, will be reborn — they cannot rest until they learn where they belong.
- **Some deliberately do not look.** A goliath who feels they have much left to resolve in
  the world may knowingly refuse to search, fearing that once they find their place among
  the stars, they will not be reborn to carry on. Rare, but not unheard of.

Two more sayings grow from this:

> *"A lifetime spent finding your place in the sky."*

Outsiders understand it easily: searching for who you are, knowing yourself. To a goliath
it is also literal — learning where their soul exists among the stars.

> *"Constellations are confusing to you?"*

Meaning: *you aren't bothering to search the sky for your soul, because you aren't ready to
leave this world.* Whether or not it's true of the listener, it's how a goliath says
*you're stuck on something.* It is kind on its face and quietly insulting underneath —
*you're obsessed, and you've lost sight of what matters.* Deeply passive-aggressive
depending on context; it says a great deal in very few words about who they are as a
people.

#### Sharing Your Star — and the Worst Insults
Whether to share is **up to the individual.** Some proudly point to their star. Others
keep it to themselves. Lying about a public claim is **unthinkable** — the weight it
carries (see *The Unforgivable*) puts it beyond the pale. Unthinkable is not impossible: it
has happened, rarely, and it is a scandal people talk about for years. Far more often, a
goliath is simply **mistaken**, and claims a star that belongs to someone else.

That is the root of the deepest insult one goliath can give another:

> *"That nightly star of yours — that is someone else's."*

It says *you are lying to yourself.* Even if the target has never claimed a star, the
intent lands: *your opinion doesn't matter, because you don't know yourself — and worse,
you've lied about which star is yours.* If they **have** shared their star, it is doubly
bad: an outright accusation of lying.

So goliaths don't always share, in case they were mistaken.

- **The elders are the ones who usually share.** They are out of politics and past their
  prime; they don't care if someone disputes them.
- **The old often become obsessed with their star** near the end — depending on whether
  they feel *complete*. Some are not, and want to be reborn.

The insult takes many forms, tuned to how hard it should land. The hardest:

> *"You can stop looking at the nightly sky."*

*You are not up there.* It is the biggest fighting words between goliaths: it literally
says *you have no afterlife; you are not part of the sky.* No goliath truly believes
another of their kind is absent from the sky — so it is almost never meant literally. It
is reserved for real anger, and understood the way "fuck you" is understood: not an
instruction, just anger everyone recognizes.

### The Unforgivable
**Murdering a goliath before they found their star.**

#### Purgatory
The goliaths believe a soul that is **strongly tied to the world** — through the people it
loves, the cause it serves — will be reborn to finish what it started. A soul that **knows
its star** goes home to the sky.

A soul with neither — young, free, no strong cause, no star yet — is at risk of being lost
to **purgatory**: somewhere between realms, not in the sky and not on earth, wandering the
spirit land because it was neither connected to the world nor to itself.

#### The Trial
When a goliath is killed, the druids gather. Before any talk of punishment, they settle
two questions, in order:

1. **Did the dead likely know their star?**
2. **How strongly were they tied to the world** — through the people they knew and the
   things they cared about?

Only then do they weigh the killer. The answers set everything:

- **Kill an elder who knew their star**, and it is a slap on the hand. From the goliath
  perspective you did not murder them; you more or less sent them home.
- **If the dead will likely be reborn or reach the sky**, the killing — murder or
  accident — can be treated as lightly as jaywalking.
- **Kill someone young, free, and starless**, and you may have condemned them to
  purgatory. That is the worst crime there is.

#### The Punishment
**Eye for an eye: locked away from the sky, underground.** You kept someone from returning
to the sky, so you will be kept from it too. It is the worst of the worst — worse than
death. Sentences run for years, scaled to the crime.

Beyond that lies a punishment that is **known of but never carried out**: at death the
condemned is **not burned**. The body is preserved by magic and buried, so the soul is held
in it, unable to return. It is taboo in itself. It is threatened, never used — though
legend says one truly evil goliath may have earned it. *(Lore to come.)*

> *"A deep grave for you."*

To an outsider it sounds like a death threat. Between goliaths it means something far
darker: *I will lock your soul away from the afterlife.* They throw it at outsiders easily;
said to a goliath, it is a far stronger insult than any threat to kill.

#### Declaring Your Star — Consent
Because of the trial, **publicly naming your star is a legal and moral statement**: *if
someone kills me by accident, they bear no punishment.* It means the speaker is
**confident** in who they are and **comfortable** with death — not necessarily ready, and
not necessarily old. Young goliaths can declare too.

Imagine going hunting with a friend, knowing that if they accidentally shoot you there is
no punishment. Would you tell the whole city, with total certainty, which star is yours?
Only if you trusted them. That is why lying about it is unthinkable — and why calling
someone a liar about it is such an enormous accusation — and why the claim is never made
lightly — it is a large statement about self and identity.

- **Small children claiming a star is foolish**, true or not. They are too young to know
  for certain, and the elders want to be sure a child truly found their star before
  freeing anyone around them from carelessness.
- **Reckless teens declare too** — two youths about to do something stupid both announce
  their stars, so neither is responsible if the stunt kills the other. An insurance policy.
  **No one takes a teen's declaration as fact.** The elders see it for what it is — kids
  covering themselves — and punish the stunt the way anyone punishes teens doing stupid
  things. Like two kids agreeing to race carts: neither expects the other to get hurt, and
  both want to be free of the blame if it happens.

So a star is not hidden, but it is not casually shared. You name it only when you are
completely confident and completely comfortable. There are edge cases.

### The Ideal
What the goliaths admire most is **complete confidence in which star is yours** — and
being **completely comfortable with joining the sky.** No doubts.

The most admirable thing a goliath can do is be **young, know their star, and declare it
publicly** — not as a teenage stunt, but with real confidence. Certainty about who you are,
early in life, is the highest virtue on Lone Peak.

That is exactly where the temptation to lie comes from. When nothing in your society says
more about you than knowing your star, claiming it before you truly know is a
tempting shortcut. The virtue and the scandal come from the same place.

### Learning to Fly
There is **no formal school.** Goliaths learn from other goliaths, the way druids think
about everything — naturally.

Some children simply get it, like birds pushed out of the nest: they shift and fly without
being taught. Once some of them are flying, the rest are pushed to follow. Some figure it
out on their own; others learn by watching. It is all very natural.

### Visitors
Anyone who reaches the summit is **welcomed as an honored guest** — and met with shock.
Their home is weeks of climbing up the steepest cliffs, through thin air, extreme cold and
huge winds, where few make it alive. Someone who came all that way to them deserves to be
greeted like it.

Some goliaths are awestruck that a visitor climbed it; others are impressed or less so
depending on whether they truly climbed or came up by magic. Either way, **they are not
xenophobic.**

#### Souls Are Not Bound to Race
Nothing in their belief ties a soul to the goliath form. They believe they are goliaths
because that form is adapted to the mountain. A goliath soul reborn among other peoples
would simply be born as one of them.

So if someone of another race climbed all the way up and claimed to know their star, they
would be welcomed. It could be one of their own, reborn. It could be someone's grandfather.
Or not — all they know is that it could be.

#### Searching the World for the Chosen
That means **the chosen leader could be born to another race.** It has never happened, but
it could. If a new star appears and no goliath child is born on the mountain that day,
they would go searching far and wide.

The world is too large to check every newborn, so the search is **passive and long**:
search parties travel from major city to major city, reading the news, looking for signs.
The chosen has always been a natural leader, with druidry coming easily. So they watch
towns large and small for someone who is changing the world — hero types. Because every
chosen is a genuinely new soul, they cannot look for a personality. They look for
**impact**, and for strong ties to druidic magic, to nature and to the sky.

### Names
- **The people: the Groundstruck** *(chosen)*. Like lightning striking the ground: each of
  them is a star that struck the earth. It speaks of arriving, not falling — they do not see
  themselves as fallen, but as here on this plane to discover who they are. ("Starfallen"
  was rejected for exactly that reason.) Lowlanders who climb the Peak leave *starstruck*;
  the goliaths are *groundstruck*.
- **Lone Peak, in their words: Ulearos** (oo-leh-AR-ohs) *(chosen)*. An Ancient name, built
  on Ao Sathanum's naming patterns — the vowel-heavy `Ulea-`, the pure proto-root `ar`, the
  ancient ending `-os`. The goliaths kept the city's name without knowing what it means.
- **The chosen leader:** The Unremembered · The New Star · Firstlight · The Unwritten
