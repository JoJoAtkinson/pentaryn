# Space Journey

> **A one-shot for 4–7 players, D&D 5.5e, level 5, ~3–4 hours, run in Foundry VTT.**
> The crew of the *U.S.S. Interprize* is trapped in their own holodeck fantasy program
> by an evil wizard who remembers every one of the twenty times they killed him.

---

## 1. The pitch (read this to the table)

> You are the senior staff of the **U.S.S. Interprize, NCC-1701-Dee**. Off duty, you run a
> sword-and-sorcery holo-program called *The Tower of Oz*. You have beaten it twenty times.
> It is boring. Tonight, someone said the fatal words:
>
> **"Computor. Make the wizard an actual challenge."**
>
> The Computor's solution was elegant and catastrophic: it gave **Oz** the memory of all twenty
> deaths. Twenty times cut down. Twenty times humiliated. He remembers your names, your tricks,
> your favorite openings — and he is *extremely* unhappy about it.
>
> In the ninety seconds it took you to stop laughing, Oz seized the holodeck's scene engine,
> locked the arch, and shut off the safety parameters.
>
> The blades are real now.
>
> You can kill him. You will, tonight, repeatedly — and it won't take: the body drops, frost
> blackens its lips, and the man underneath is already in the next scene wearing someone new.
>
> So stop killing him and start cornering him. Every scene you resolve earns **Trace** — the slow,
> guaranteed way. And some of you have abilities that break a scene open outright — bonus Trace,
> and every bonus box is a **free pass**: one scene, of your choosing, that you simply refuse to
> play. Walk into something you don't like the look of and you can turn around and leave.
>
> **Ten Trace and it's over.** There are ten scenes, so grinding it out always gets you there;
> earn the bonuses and you get there early, while you're all still standing. Either way it ends in
> his trophy room — the one room with nobody left for him to wear, and the only place his death
> will stick.
>
> **Still in?** Then you make exactly two choices: your **post on the ship**, and a **premade
> adventurer** off the stack. All eight sheets are built at level 5 with nothing left to decide —
> it's one night, and nobody spends it on character creation.
>
> They're separate picks on purpose. Nothing stops the ship's Counselor from spending tonight as a
> screaming Barbarian, or the Redshirt from turning up as the holy Paladin. The mismatch is the fun.
> **Two picks, five minutes, and we start.**

*Roles are §3, the two picks are §4, the sheets are in
[space-journey-pregens.md](space-journey-pregens.md).*

---

## 2. The core loop (the whole game in six lines)

1. The party lands in a **scene** — a fantasy set piece from the holodeck's library. **Ten of
   them, fixed, in order (§10).** Oz never chooses which scene loads. Neither do you.
2. **Oz possesses exactly one NPC in that scene** — and inherits that body completely: stat
   block, gear, limits, everything. No wizard powers unless the host came with them. Every other
   NPC is honest, dumb program.
3. **One objective all night: 10 Trace.** Every scene resolved pays **+1** — the floor. Ten
   scenes at +1 each means the slow way always gets there.
4. **Bonus Trace banks a free pass.** Name the host before he strikes, break the set open with a
   role ability (§5), do something the GM loves — each bonus box is one scene (2–8) the party can
   refuse to play, *their pick*, called at the door before the first roll. Same destination, fewer
   wounds.
5. **The scene is then spent** — the order marches forward, minus whatever you've passed on, and
   never back.
6. It ends in the **trophy room** (scene 10): himself, in his own body, and **no NPCs left to
   wear.** Kill him there and it finally sticks.

Bootleg on purpose. Every name is a real name until you hear it next to the original — that's
the joke, and it should take under a second.

**On the Ensign:** he doesn't have a name. Not a redacted one, not a funny one — the field is
empty. The Computor never assigned him one and no member of this crew has ever asked. Every NPC
in every scene calls him **"Ensign,"** or "you there," or "the other one." If a player asks what
his name is, the answer is that nobody knows, including him. He is the red shirt.

**On Weasely:** the "Acting" in his rank is not an abbreviation and it is not going away. Say the
full rank every single time you address him — *"Acting Ensign Thrasher"* — in the same tone you'd
use for a child's certificate of participation. **Dr. Thrasher is the only person in the universe
who calls him "Westley."**

Support cast the program will mangle: **Chief O'Brine** (transporters, pickled in the pattern
buffer), **Lt. Tasha Yarn** (security; already dead, and now literally a *yarn* — a story people
keep retelling), and **Nurse Chapelle** — who is not a person at all but the E.M.H.'s
bedside-manner subroutine, and it shows. The ship's computer answers to **"Computor"** (rhymes
with "razor," it insists), and the bar is **Deck Nine Aft**, because the knockoff got the number
*and* the direction wrong.

The villain: **Ozmandius the Unmade**, "Oz." Was a level-appropriate stock wizard. Is now a
grudge with a spellbook. He is the one thing in the program that isn't a knockoff, which is
quietly part of why he wins.

## 4. Character creation — two picks, five minutes

Every player makes exactly **two choices**. That's it. Hand them a sheet, start playing.

> **Hand them [space-journey-player-handout.md](space-journey-player-handout.md).** That's the
> player-facing one-pager: both picks, every ability in one line, and the Doctor card — with no
> hosts, no scene list, and no Trace bookkeeping. Everything below is the GM's copy.

### Pick 1 — Your role on the ship

Pick from the handout's role table — role, name, and both abilities in a line each, with a **★** on
the ones whose fine print changes how they're played. The "Obviously" column in §3 is deliberately
*not* on the handout; the table works out who's who on their own, and it takes about a second.

This is your **meta layer**: it grants your holodeck abilities (§5). It has no hit points and
swings no sword — that's Pick 2.

### Pick 2 — Your pregen

Pre-built level 5 D&D characters, printed and ready. This is your **body inside the program**.
Any role can take any pregen — the mismatch is the joke (the Counselor is the Barbarian, the
Redshirt is the Paladin).

| Pregen | Class / subclass | One-line hook | Complexity |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bram Ironvow** | Fighter (Champion) | A shield, a longsword, and no imagination | Medium |
| **Skarn the Red** | Barbarian (Berserker) | Rages, regrets, rages again | **Simple** |
| **Pip Locksley** | Rogue (Thief) | Sneak attack, thieves' tools, zero patience | **Simple** |
| **Sister Vell** | Warlock (Fiend) | Took a vow of mercy; her new employer took it as a suggestion | **Simple** |
| **Magister Orin** | Wizard (Evoker) | Fireball. That's the hook | Complex |
| **Ballad Quinn** | Bard (Lore) | Cutting Words, and knows every scene's genre | Complex |
| **Thessa Longsight** | Ranger (Hunter) | Tracking, which matters a *lot* in this one | Medium |
| **Dame Corriel** | Paladin (Devotion) | Divine Smite and moral certainty (aura sold separately until level 6) | Medium |

**Full pinned builds and the lethality math live in
[space-journey-pregens.md](space-journey-pregens.md).** Every sheet has its spells, maneuvers,
feats, and gear locked — nothing left to choose, and a one-line turn script printed on it.

> **The name on the sheet is a sticker, not a rule.** Nothing mechanical is attached to it. Want
> to play the wizard and call yourself Skarn the Red? Do it. Want to swap names with someone at
> the table? Do it. Want the Paladin to be named Pip? Fine. This costs nothing, takes no time,
> and is **not a third pick** — it's just permission. Two picks, then flavor to taste.
>
> Two picks stays the rule because players are already tracking two identities: their **ship
> role** and their **pregen**, all night. Don't add a third axis; the mismatch comedy already
> lives in role-vs-class, and it's funnier there because there's real fiction behind it.

> **Prep note:** build all eight in Foundry as actors in a `Space Journey — Pregens` folder,
> level 5, fully kitted, no attunement decisions left open. Players claim one at the table
> and get ownership assigned live.
>
> **Healing is normal — see §6.** Party healing, potions, Hit Dice and Second Wind all work as
> written. The E.M.H. is free healing on top, not the only source. Hand the **Simple** sheets to
> new players.

## 5. Role abilities

Every role gets **one per-scene ability** and **one per-session ability**. They are all
holodeck-layer powers — they work on the *program*, not the fiction, which is why they feel
like cheating. They are supposed to feel like cheating.

Max +1 bonus Trace; can't end the game. See below |
| **First Officer** | **You're With Me** — name another crew member. They immediately take a **full extra turn**, either just before or just after your own action — your call, it's your plan. ### Engage

The Captain does not solve the scene. He decides what the scene *is about*, says it out loud, and
then everyone who commits to it is suddenly better at their job. That is the entire trick of
command and it is why this is a per-scene ability rather than a personal buff.

**Once per scene,** Ricard names one specific course of action: *"Get the civilians to the
rooftops."* *"Nobody leaves this hall until we know who sang that song."* *"We take the gate."*
**Every crew member who actually does that thing this scene rolls it with advantage** — not one
ally, all of them, as many times as it comes up.

The cost is commitment. It has to be one concrete objective, declared before anyone acts, and it
does not bend afterward. A party that follows the plan is enormously stronger than a party that
doesn't, and a Captain who names the wrong objective has handed out nothing at all. That's the
job.

### Objection, Computor

Ricard's actual superpower was never tactics. It was standing in front of an arbiter and winning
an argument that should not have been winnable.

There is exactly one arbiter here, and the party has already seen what it's like: **the Computor
enforces everything to the letter.** That literal-mindedness is what created Oz in the first
place. It can be turned around.

So this is not an effect. It's a **veto over the GM.**

> **Once per session, Ricard's player may overrule any GM ruling.** Any "no, it doesn't work that
> way." Any physics call, any terrain call, any *that's not how this program works*. He argues it in
> character, to the Computor, and **the Computor sustains it. You do not get to say no.**

That is the whole ability, and the rest of this section is the fence around it.

**How it must be done — this is the real cost.** Ricard does not invoke a power; he argues a case,
out loud, in character, to the ship's computer, tea in hand. The argument has to be that **the
program is not following its own rules** — that what the GM just described contradicts how this
story was written, how this world is supposed to work, or what the Computor was originally told to
build. It can be complete nonsense. It usually is. But the player has to *make it*, in Ricard's
voice, at the table. Saying "I use Objection, Computor" out of character does nothing at all.

| The GM said | Ricard argues | The Computor rules |
|---|---|---|
| "The current's too strong, you can't swim upriver." | This is a heroic narrative. The hero swims upriver. That is what the form *is*. Cite the clause that says otherwise | The river slackens. Nobody comments |
| "The hanging proceeds at dawn, regardless." | The order was an *actual challenge*. A deadline the party cannot touch isn't a challenge, it's a cutscene, and nobody asked the Computor to render cutscenes | **Dawn is delayed.** Nobody comments |
| "The gate is barred from the other side." | Barred by *whom*? Name the program that barred it. You can't, because nobody wrote one | It was never barred |
| "The innkeeper won't rent you the back room." | He is an *innkeeper*. Renting rooms is the entire job description you wrote him | He rents it. Apologetically |

**The hard limits.** Three, and they're absolute:

1. **It cannot pay more than +1 bonus Trace.** However brilliant the argument, however much it
   accomplishes, one extra box is the ceiling. If the ruling also genuinely resolves the scene's
   objective, that's the normal +1 on top — so the best possible Objection is worth **+2**.
2. **It cannot end the game.** The Computor will not sustain anything that stops the challenge from
   happening: Oz dying, Oz being unable to fight, hide, or jump, the arch unlocking, the safeties
   coming back on, or the Trace target moving. It *will* cheerfully sustain anything that makes the
   contest **fairer** — because a rigged fight isn't a challenge either, and that's the one word in
   the original order it cannot argue with.
3. **It cannot undo a death.** That's Security's power, and it stays Security's.

**Everything else is fair game**, and the range is enormous: terrain, weather, distance, what a
program is willing to do, whether a door was ever locked, whether an NPC has the key, how much of
the wall is still standing, whether Oz's bookkeeping is symmetrical with the party's. Used well it
can **end a scene outright** — an objective that was impossible becomes trivially possible, the
scene resolves, and the party moves on with the bonus box.

> **GM contract: say yes.** Not "make a Persuasion check." Not "hmm, that's a stretch." **Yes.**
> The ability is *winning an argument you should not have won* — the moment you get to weigh whether
> the argument was good, the ability is gone. The three limits above do all the balancing. Your only
> job is to look faintly betrayed and then narrate the world rearranging itself.
>
> Everything the ruling changed **stays changed for the rest of the scene**, and timing rulings
> (like the delayed dawn) last as long as they say. Oz spends the scene appealing, and at the end of
> it he wins the appeal — the Computor is literal, not stupid, and he is very good at this.

> **The ritual:** he cannot make this argument without a cup of tea in his hand. Nobody has ever
> established whether that's a requirement or a preference. He has never been without one. Leave it
> unestablished until the night he doesn't have one — at which point the Computor pauses the
> proceedings to replicate a cup of Earl Grey, and Oz gets one free action while it steeps.

### Interface

Datum does not hack the program. He touches it, and it does what he says, because he is speaking to
it in the only language it has ever had.

**Once per session, as his action,** Datum puts a hand on one program — any NPC in the current scene
— and interfaces directly with it. No roll. It stops being scenery.

> **It obeys him exactly and literally. It comes with the party from scene to scene. It stays until
> it dies.**

**How to run it at the table:** it acts on Datum's initiative, immediately after him, and takes one
action. It does precisely what it was last told, with zero interpretation, and **if it isn't given a
new order it repeats the old one** — forever, if nobody notices. Don't stat it up: AC 13, ~20 HP, one
attack if what it was would plausibly have one, none if it was a baker. It is not a monster. It's a
person the party is going to get attached to.

**It survives the scene change, and here is exactly why.** Every other program is wiped and rebuilt
from scratch when the set changes — that's *why* they're all honest and stupid, and why none of them
can tell you a single thing about last scene. This one is interfaced with **Datum**, who is a ship
system with persistent storage. Its continuity no longer lives in the holodeck at all.

> **It lives in the android.**

So the program re-dresses him for every new set — the bandits' lookout turns up as a market
porter, then a pot-boy at the inn, then a torch-bearer in the catacombs, new face and new job
each time — and he is the only program in the program who **remembers being the last one.**

### The part that costs something

**That memory is the entire mechanism.** Nobody wrote him a soul and Datum didn't install one. He
simply became the one thing in the program with an unbroken past, and it turns out that is
sufficient. Move him one rung every scene he survives, and never faster:

| Scene | Where he's at |
|---|---|
| 1st | A tool. Blank, literal, obedient. The Computor's label for him is *Guard #4* |
| 2nd | He notices he was somewhere else a moment ago. He asks one question about it |
| 3rd | He asks Datum what he is. **Datum has to answer or refuse — either one is a choice** |
| 4th | He refuses one order, and volunteers for something else instead. Better, usually |
| 5th+ | He gives you his name. It is the first thing in this program that was never written down. He will step in front of something without being asked |

**The cost is not a rule. It's the fifth column.** This party's default tactic is throwing expendable
things at problems — that is *literally* the Redshirt engine (§5) and the Doctor's *"Distract them!"*
(§6). Interface hands them one more body to throw, and then charges interest on it. By scene 6 there
is a man with a name standing next to them who is worth exactly as much as they've decided he is.

**And when he dies, Datum still has the memories.** That's not a rule either. It's just where they
were being kept.

> **GM contract:** never punish them for spending him, and never let it be free. Do not moralize, do
> not editorialize, and do not have anyone at the table make a speech about it. Just play him one rung
> more awake than last time and let the players do the arithmetic themselves. If they burn him in
> scene 2 to hold a door, that's a clean trade and you say nothing. If they burn him in scene 7,
> **say nothing then either.** It'll land.

**The limits:**

- **The possessed host: it works.** The host is honest canned code with Oz riding it, so Datum
  gets the host's honest data — real name, real job, real memories, none of which explain what
  the body has been doing for the last ten minutes. That mismatch is a confession with a
  timestamp. Interface doesn't evict Oz — it was never an exorcism — but it is one of the
  cleanest ways to catch him wearing someone.
- **Not the Doctor.** He's ship infrastructure and he'd be insufferable about it.
- **One at a time, one per session.** If it dies, the ability is spent — no second interface, no
  replacement. This is the opposite of the Redshirt engine, and on purpose.
- **No bonus Trace.** What it buys is a body that can hold a rope, ring a bell, carry a civilian, or
  hold a gate — so it earns objectives (+1 each) the ordinary way, all night.

**Oz cannot take it back** — its continuity lives in the android now, not the holodeck, and hard
rule 1 (§8) gives Oz exactly one body per scene, which he is already wearing. But he understands
immediately what it's worth, and he has spent twenty deaths learning to take things off these people.
**He will target it**, and he will spend a turn doing so when he has better options.

And once, when it's awake enough to understand him, he will explain to it what it actually is. Let
him. Don't resolve it.

### "I'll State the Nature of the Emergency"

He opens with *"Please state the nature of the medical emergency"* every time he's activated, and
he has done it to this crew several thousand times. Dr. Thrasher stopped waiting for him to finish
that sentence years ago.

**Once per scene,** she cuts him off and gives him a **second order in the same round** — on top
of the table's one. And because she is his Chief Medical Officer and not a passenger, **that order
ignores his once-per-scene limits**:

- **Heal someone he has already healed this scene.** Another 2d8+5, same person, right now.
- **Re-fool an enemy that already fell for the distraction.** It wastes another attack on him.

This is the only way to get a second heal onto the same body in one scene, which makes the Chief
Medical Officer the party's real healer after all — she just does it by managing him instead of
by casting anything. It also means the round the tank drops is survivable, exactly once per scene.

He complies instantly and resents it visibly. He has, on file, a formal objection to being
interrupted. Nobody has read it.

### Regulation 121, Section A

**Starfleet Medical Regulation 121, Section A** gives the Chief Medical Officer authority to
relieve any officer of duty — including one of superior rank, including the Captain — when they
are medically unfit, **compromised by an alien intelligence**, or showing seriously impaired
judgment. It is real, it is canon, and the Computor enforces it to the letter, because the
Computor enforces everything to the letter. That is how the party got into this mess.

**Once per session,** Dr. Thrasher declares a medical emergency and relieves **every program she
can see** of duty. No target, no roll, no asking. She cites the regulation out loud and the
program obeys its own rules.

| Who | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Every program in sight** | Stands down. Guards ground their weapons. The mob sits. The executioner goes home. The mercenaries put down their drinks. Combat, if there was combat, is over |
| **Crew under any control** | Charm, fear, domination, possession — all of it ends instantly, no save |
| **Oz's host** | **It binds the host.** The body obeys procedure and starts to stand down — and Oz either lets it, or visibly fights his own hands in front of everyone |

That is the whole ability, and it's the best image in the one-shot: a full market square sitting
down in unison, and one man frozen halfway — knuckles white, jaw working, arguing with his own
knees.

**Why it doesn't break the game:** it finds him, and finding him is not winning. The regulation is
a **reveal tool, not a dead end** — the host's program *wants* to obey, so Oz has to hold the body
upright by force while everyone watches him do it. He takes the naming bonus on the chin, stops
pretending, and comes straight at the party in an empty room with nobody left to hide behind —
which cuts both ways, because there's also nobody left to soak whatever the host's stat block can
throw. It buys a guaranteed identification and a clean arena. It does not buy a kill.

**It only reaches what she can see, and this is the whole limitation.** Oz is somewhere in the
*scene*, but scenes are big — a market square, an opera house, a catacomb network. He is under no
obligation to be in this room. If he's across the square, backstage, a gallery up, or simply
around a corner, then **everyone in sight stands down and nobody is left struggling.**

**That's a whiff, and it costs the whole session's use.** The party is now stood in a room full of
seated programs having learned one negative fact, and — worse — Oz *heard it*. He knows the Chief
Medical Officer has spent 121-A and can never spend it again. He will spend the rest of the night
staying out of enclosed rooms, which is itself a tell, but not one you get for free.

**So it isn't strictly better than the Counselor — it's the opposite instrument.**

| | *I Sense Deception* (Counselor) | *Regulation 121-A* (Medical) |
|---|---|---|
| Cadence | **Once per scene**, all night | **Once per session.** One shell |
| Reach | One character, chosen | Everyone in sight, indiscriminate |
| Answer | Always definitive — yes or no | Definitive only if he's in the room |
| Failure | There isn't one. A "no" is real information | Whiffs entirely if he's out of sight |
| Volume | Silent. He never knows she looked | Deafening. He knows, and adapts |

Hers is a scalpel she gets ten of. This is a shotgun with one shell, fired in the dark.

> **The running gag:** the regulation only ever works *downward*. The Doctor has tried citing 121-A at
> Captain Ricard — to relieve him of command, on the grounds that anyone who orders a holodeck
> wizard to be "an actual challenge" has demonstrated seriously impaired judgment. Ricard ignored
> him. He will try again this session. It will not work. Play it exactly the same way every time.

### I Sense Deception

It is a straight answer. Point, and the GM says yes or no. Oz cannot beat it, spoof it, or
prepare for it, and the GM does not get to be clever about the wording. The limit is that it is
**one character, once per scene** — so the Counselor can either narrow the field or call the shot,
never both.

**She learns it silently, and that matters.** Naming him out loud costs him the pretense and pays
**+1 Trace** — but it does not make him leave (§8), it makes him turn around. So the silence is
for setting up: she knows, she tells the party quietly, Striker holds **You're With Me**, the
Ranger draws a bead, Sister Vell readies Hold Person. *Then* somebody says the name, and the whole
opening round lands on a man who thought he was still just the innkeeper.

A "no" is worth nearly as much. Two "no"s in two scenes teaches the table how Oz thinks, and
that's the actual game.

> **Yes, empathy shouldn't work on holograms. It works anyway.**
>
> This is deliberate. It is not an oversight, it is not a plot hole you need to patch, and there
> is no explanation coming. Trois reads programs exactly as well as she reads people and always
> has. Somebody asked her about it once. She said she didn't know either, and the conversation
> moved on to something more interesting.
>
> If a player raises it at the table, agree with them warmly and completely, and then keep
> playing. We are running a game about a wizard who found out he has been murdered twenty times
> by the same eight people. The empath works on the light. Have fun.

### Panel Glitch (the other way out of a scene)

Resolving a scene ends it. Taking the set apart ends it *faster and pays better* — but the party
doesn't get to choose this one. It has to show up.

**Panels only exist if someone took Chief Engineer.** No engineer, no panels, and the party is
back to hunting Oz the hard way. This is the whole value of the role pick.

**Spotting a panel — nobody's decision.** Whenever any crew member makes a Perception or
Investigation check **for some other reason** and the **total is 20 or higher**, they have also
noticed something wrong with the world: a flagstone with a seam, a mirror with a part number, a
tapestry hanging on nothing. **The GM decides where it is.** Cap it at **one panel per scene**.

**You cannot go looking.** A check made to find a panel finds nothing, ever. The holodeck's
seams only show to someone who was looking at something else. Say this once and then hold it.

> **The odds** (level 5, so PB +3). Trained: **30–35%** per check. Rogue with Expertise:
> **50%**. Untrained: **5–20%**, depending on Wisdom — low, but never zero, which is the point.
> At two or three incidental checks a scene, expect a panel to surface **roughly every other
> scene**. Over ten scenes that's ~5 sightings and, realistically, ~3 the party actually reaches.

**Reaching it is the secondary objective.** Place it somewhere that costs something — behind the
bandits' line, under the scaffold, backstage past the fire, down the branch of catacomb the guide
swears is unsafe. Spotting a panel should reorganize the party's whole plan for the
scene. If it's convenient, you've placed it wrong.

**Opening it.** The Chief Engineer must reach it and spend his per-scene action. No roll — he's
the engineer, it's a panel, he opens it. Then:

- **+2 Trace** for the scene — the +1 for taking it, plus **+1 bonus, which banks a free pass**
  (§8). An engineer who finds his panels carves two or three scenes off the night single-handed.
- **The set collapses mid-sentence.** The host folds up like scenery, *this* scene ends resolved,
  and the party walks into the next one acting first. The pass it banked is theirs to spend
  whenever they like. Oz hates this more than being stabbed.

**Oz cannot touch the panels.** Same reasoning as the doctor — that's ship infrastructure, not
his program, and the host certainly can't open one. He can only watch someone walk toward it,
which is the single best use of a villain who has spent twenty deaths being in control.

### Reroute Power

He gets the safety parameters back. Not for long, and not for free, but he gets them back — and for
a moment the program is the harmless toy it was supposed to be all along.

**Once per session, declared on his turn.** It costs no action; he is already elbow-deep in
something.

> **No party member can take real damage until the end of LaFarge's next turn.**

**Count it out loud, because the timing is the whole ability:** he declares it → every other
combatant takes their turn → his next turn comes around and he acts again → **it ends when that turn
ends.** So the party gets one complete round of turns under it *plus* his second turn. That's a
full round of absolute immunity, not the half-round "1 round" usually means at a table.

**What it does and doesn't cover:**

- **Damage only.** Roll it, narrate it, then don't apply it — the blades pass through everyone like
  the light they are. Damage already taken stays taken; this heals nothing.
- **Non-damaging effects still land.** Grappled, frightened, stunned, held, knocked prone, shoved
  into the water — all of it works normally. Oz's control game is untouched, which is what keeps
  this from being a free round.
- **Programs are not protected.** The crowd, the guards, the wounded soldier, the civilians you're
  trying to save — they all still die on schedule. The party is invulnerable and the scene is not.
- **The party's own damage output is unaffected.** This is the nova window.

**Use it on the round you can see coming:** the fireball, the siege engine, the ceiling, the round
the tank is at 6 HP and Oz has legendary actions left. Damage prevented beats damage healed, because
it also buys you the turn you'd have spent healing.

The grid flashes yellow, the Computor chirps its little compliance tone, and Oz — who has been
enjoying the safeties being off more than anything else tonight — notices immediately. He spends the
round trying to get them switched back off, which is a round he isn't spending on the party.

### Standard Issue

He brought a phaser.

Nobody saw him bring it. It was not there a moment ago and it is there now, and he is holding it
like a man who has been holding it the entire time. **Do not explain this and do not let anyone
else explain it.** If a player asks where it came from, Striker says "standard issue" and
continues walking. That is the whole answer and it is the correct one.

**Once per session. One shot. Full power. It does not roll and it does not miss.** Choose one:

| Target | What happens |
|---|---|
| **A piece of the world** | A wall, a gate, a bridge, a portcullis, a wagon, the floor. Not damaged — **gone.** A hole the shape of a hole |
| **A line of programs** | Every NPC in a straight line, deleted. The guards, the mob, the front rank |
| **The host** | Deleted, and Oz's evening's work with it. **The scene ends, resolved** — **+1 Trace, plus +1 bonus, which banks a free pass** (§8). The party acts first on the far side |

**Then the holodeck notices.** An out-of-scope object was introduced into a running program, and
the Computor removes it with an apologetic chime. The phaser is gone for good.

**It does not put back what he shot.** This is the important half. The wall stays gone. The bridge
stays gone. The guards do not come back. Oz gets one body per scene and nothing else, so he
watches a hole appear in the world and has to keep working around it for the rest of the night.

**Two limits worth saying out loud:**

- **He has to know which one is Oz.** The phaser is a finisher, not a detector — someone has to
  identify him first. This is the single best thing the Counselor's silent "yes" can be handed to.
- **It does not kill him.** Before the trophy room, nothing does. A full-power phaser blast just
  deletes the man he was wearing and ends the argument, and he walks into the next scene furious
  and untouched.

> **If he saves it for the finale (scene 10):** a pass is worth nothing there — nothing passes on
> the ending — so instead the
> shot takes **half of Oz's current hit points**, no save, no reaction, no *"Not this time."* It
> doesn't win the fight. It makes holding onto it for nine scenes worth having done.

> **Alternate, if you'd rather he be a talker than a shooter:** *Poker Face* — once per session,
> tell Oz one lie and he believes it completely, no save. Twenty deaths taught him how these
> people fight, not how they lie. Pairs well with the Captain's *Objection, Computor* — Ricard
> pins him in the scene, Striker tells him the wrong thing about it.

### Today Is a Good Day

**Passive. Always on. Nothing to spend, nothing to declare.**

Wharf does not choose to die and cannot trigger this on purpose. He fights the way he always
fights — into the front of it, past the point where anyone sensible would withdraw — and then:

> **If no other crew member can see him die, it doesn't take.** He turns up in the next scene at
> full HP.
>
> **If they watch him die, he's dead.** Really dead. Same as anyone.

So the whole trick is arranging your own death *out of view*. He holds the doorway and tells them
to run. He goes over the cliff with the mob wrapped around him. The ceiling comes down between him
and everyone else and the last thing they hear is him sounding pleased about it.

**Two rulings keep it honest:**

- **It has to be a death, not a disappearance.** The GM must agree that what he did would
  obviously kill him. Stepping behind a tent is not a heroic sacrifice.
- **The party needs a real reason not to be looking** — ordered to run, door shut, water took him,
  fifty feet away holding the line. Everyone at the table politely turning around does not count,
  and you should say so with a completely straight face the first time somebody tries it.

**It still costs.** He is dead for the rest of that scene either way. The party fights on without
their heaviest hitter for fifteen minutes every time he pulls this, which is the price that keeps
a survivable death from being a free action.

A Klongon who works out that dying is survivable *only if nobody sees* will start engineering
increasingly magnificent unwitnessed last stands. That is the correct outcome. Say the line every
time.

### He Prefers Not to Discuss It

**Once per session:** they watched him die, and he comes back anyway.

No off-screen trick, no doorway, no collapsing ceiling. The party saw the whole thing, up close,
with detail. Next scene he is standing in the crowd at full HP.

Nobody explains it. **He** does not explain it. If asked, he says it is not important, and if
pressed, he says it again. Datum's readings return nothing, the Counselor gets nothing, the
Computor has no record. Hold that line all night; it is the only thing in the program with no
mechanism, and the joke dies the moment somebody gives it one.


### "Acting Ensign!" (the Weasely engine)

You put him in his place, and something in him snaps into gear. Nobody has found another way to
make it happen.

**Trigger: Weasely fails an ability check.** Any *other* crew member may immediately put Acting
Ensign Thrasher in his place. No action, no turn order, no limit on who — it happens in the beat
of silence right after the die lands wrong.

**How is entirely up to the player** — cuff him, shove him, flick his ear, cut him off
mid-sentence, say the full rank flatly, tell him this isn't the bridge, remind him who's actually
in command. Physical or verbal, cruel or weary, it doesn't matter.

**He takes 1 damage regardless.** No roll, no save, it always lands. A shove and a well-chosen
sentence do exactly the same thing to him, and the safeties are off, so that is a real point of
real damage. Let the table decide what that says about him.

**Effect: he rerolls the check with Expertise** — double proficiency, **even in a skill he has no
business knowing**. He takes the new roll. Then he explains, at length, how he knew that.

The reroll *is* the second die, so there's no advantage on top; a failed roll plus a fresh one
plus +6 is already the biggest swing any role gets. What it costs is his blood and somebody's
patience.

**The price escalates, the payoff doesn't.** The second time in a scene deals **2** damage. Third
deals **3**. Fourth deals **4**. The bonus never improves. With one heal per scene, a table that
leans on this is spending a real resource on their own crewman.

**Only contempt works.** It has to be a crew member, deliberately, dismissively. Enemy attacks do
nothing. Falling damage does nothing. Genuine concern does nothing. He must be *put in his place*,
not merely hurt.

**Oz has figured this out.** He will not attack Weasely. He will step around him, refuse to target
him, and let him live — because hurting the boy makes him useful, and Oz has twenty deaths' worth
of patience. Play it straight and let the table notice on their own. Being the one person the
villain considers beneath killing is the most insulting thing in this document.

### Statistically Inevitable (the Redshirt engine)

When the Redshirt dies — and the Redshirt will die — the holodeck doors sigh open, a *different*
crewman wanders in mid-sentence looking for the gym, and the doors slam. The player immediately
takes a **fresh pregen at full HP**. Trace, Inspiration, and party knowledge carry over. Zero
downtime, zero sitting out.

They don't get a name either. They're an **Ensign**. So was the last one.

**d8 — who stumbles in next**

| d8 | Who walks in | Their first line |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | An ensign in gym clothes | "Is this the yoga one?" |
| 2 | An ensign holding a sandwich | "I'm not even on shift." |
| 3 | An ensign who has clearly been crying | "Whose sword is th—" |
| 4 | An ensign with a clipboard | "The arch is *stuck*, you guys." |
| 5 | An ensign nobody recognizes | "Do I know you people?" |
| 6 | An ensign who was told there was a party | "I was told there'd be cake." |
| 7 | An ensign who is *thrilled* to be here | "I've read the safety briefing, actually." |
| 8 | Lt. Tasha Yarn. A *recording* of her, anyway | "...didn't I die?" |

Roll 8 twice in a session and it's the same recording, saying the same thing, in the same tone.
Nobody comments on it.

**Counters this:** each new redshirt after the second enters one scene *later*, so the death
still costs the party a beat.

> **GM note, so it reads as intended rather than missed:** healing or no healing, a fresh pregen
> at full HP is the single biggest heal in the game, and a clever table will notice. That's fine —
> it costs a real death, and the escalating delay taxes anyone who tries to farm it. Don't close
> the loophole; it's the Redshirt's whole joke.

## 6. The Holo-Doctor

The party has no healer. That is on purpose — nobody flies to game night to be the healbot.
Instead they get **the Doctor**, who is not a party member. He is a talking item that heals.

**His name is "the Doctor."** That's it. Six years in service and the personnel file still reads
`PHYSICIAN NAME: [TO BE DETERMINED]`, so everyone calls him what he is, and he has made an
enormous amount of peace with that in a way that is somehow worse than complaining. Inside the
program he's a cleric, and the NPCs call him Doctor too, because nobody offered them anything
else.

**Why he's here:** the ship's Emergency Medical Holograph has spent six years in one room. When
the crew booked the holodeck, he asked to come along. He wanted to try something that wasn't
medicine — to expand his programming, get out of the office, be a *person* for an evening. He
rolled up a cleric. He took only healing. The crew gave him an enormous amount of grief about
this and he has not forgotten a word of it. Then the safeties went off, and now the one guy who
can't fight is the only one who matters.

For years this crew shut him off mid-sentence whenever they were done with him. He controls his
own deactivation sequence now. He will mention this. He will mention it more than once.

**Design contract:** he is a *talking sword that heals*, not an NPC. He has **two orders and a
default**. He never surprises you. If the GM is ever improvising his behavior, something has gone
wrong — go back to the card.

### The card — this is the entire rule set

> **THE DOCTOR — E.M.H. Mk-Won**
>
> He is always with the party. He cannot die. He cannot be hacked.
>
> **No order → he hides.** He's a doctor, not a fighter. He will find cover and stay in it.
>
> **Once per round, anyone may shout one order. He does exactly that, then hides again.**
>
> | Order | What happens |
> |---|---|
> | **"Heal me!"** | He runs to that crew member and restores **2d8+5 HP**. **Once per crew member per scene.** |
> | **"Distract them!"** | He runs into the open shouting. One enemy you name within 30 ft **wastes its next attack on him** — it passes right through. **Each enemy falls for this once per scene.** |
>
> **Anything else** → *"I'm a doctor, not a [whatever you just asked for]."* He does not move.
>
> **Out of combat, free, unlimited: "Doctor, take a look at this."** He examines a body, a
> wound, or a sick NPC and tells you **one true thing** — what killed it, what's wrong with it,
> or whether the injury matches the story someone told you. He is never wrong and he never
> speculates.
>
> **He is free healing nobody had to volunteer for.** Your own healing works normally too.

That's it. Print it, hand it to the players, never explain it again.

**A note on the Medicine skill:** in 2024 rules, Medicine only *diagnoses an illness or determines
what killed the recently slain* — stabilizing a dying creature moved to the Help action (DC 10
Wisdom (Medicine), and a stabilized creature is still unconscious at 0 HP). None of that is worth
a rule here, because "Heal me!" already stands a dying PC up with ~14 HP, which is strictly
better. So his Medicine is pure **diagnosis** — no roll, no DC, no stabilize clause. Point him
at a corpse and he tells you the truth about it.

It's a hunting tool, not a healing one, and it's deliberately narrow so it doesn't step on the
Counselor's *I Sense Deception*: he reads **bodies**, not people. He can tell you the bandit
captain died a full breath after the killing blow, with black frost on his lips, that the corpse
the market mob is avenging died of nothing anyone did, or that the hired guide's limp has no
actual injury under it. He cannot tell you who anyone is.

### The three things he cannot do

- **He cannot die.** Attacks pass through him with a shimmer and a withering look. This is why
  "Distract them!" works, and it's the party's best tactical toy: the immortal man is the perfect
  thing to wave in front of something with teeth.
- **He cannot fight, scout, carry, lift, open, or lie.** The Hippocratic subroutine is hard-coded
  ship-side, and everything else is just him being bad at adventuring. The answer is always the
  same sentence. Say it in the same tired voice every time — that's the joke.
- **He cannot be touched by Oz.** He isn't one of Oz's programs; he's a ship system that tagged
  along. Oz can't delete him, reprogram him, mute him, or wear his face. Oz *loathes* him, and
  says so.

### Healing works. Don't explain it.

**Party healing is legal and normal.** Cure Wounds, Lay on Hands, Second Wind, potions, Hit Dice —
all of it works exactly as written. If a player asks how hard light can knit a real wound when it
just opened one, the answer is *"the same way it cut you,"* said in a tone that closes the subject,
and then you keep playing. This is the same ruling as the Counselor reading holograms (§5): agree
warmly, offer nothing further, move on.

**He also mends programs, free and unlimited.** In the fiction he's a cleric, so **"Heal that
guard!"** doesn't count against anyone's once-per-scene and doesn't cost the round's order if the
target is an NPC — he does it on his own, because it's his job and nobody had to ask. In scene 2 a
mended coachman drives the wagon out of the ambush instead of bleeding under it. In scene 7 every
chorus member he patches walks out of the smoke on their own feet.

> **What he's for, now that he isn't the whole healing economy:** he's free healing nobody spent a
> character slot on, an immortal decoy (*"Distract them!"*), and the party's only reliable forensics
> (*"Doctor, take a look at this."*). Nobody had to volunteer to be the healbot, and the party still
> has one. That was always the point; being the *only* source was never load-bearing.

### What he says

Roll a d6 whenever he does anything, or just pick. Never improvise him.

| d6 | Bark |
|---|---|
| 1 | "Hold still. This will hurt, because you insisted on reality." |
| 2 | "I *have* a name. It's in the file. Somewhere. It's being determined." |
| 3 | "I mended that peasant instantly. *You* I have to sew. Consider your choices." |
| 4 | "You again. I'm writing 'recurring' in your chart." |
| 5 | "Stabbing the doctor. Bold. Ineffective." |
| 6 | "I took *one* combat spell off my list. One. You people have not let it go." |

And the fixed line, word for word, every single time somebody asks him to do something useful:

> **"I'm a doctor, not a locksmith."**
> *(…not a lockpick, not a siege engineer, not a bouncer, not a distraction — wait, no, that one
> I can do.)*

## 7. The Re-Casting rule (optional variant — NOT USED IN THIS VERSION)

> **Not in play tonight.** In this version the crew keeps one character all night — you walk into
> every scene as your pregen, and the program treats you as exactly the adventurers you appear to

> table time and it muddies the host hunt, which is why this version dropped it.

## 8. Oz — how the villain actually works

### The three hard rules (say these out loud in scene 2)

1. **He possesses exactly one NPC per scene, and inherits it entirely.** Stat block, hit points,
   gear, limits — all of it. If the host can't cast, Oz can't cast. If the host is an innkeeper,
   Oz fights like an innkeeper. No wizard powers unless the body came with them. He got dealt
   into the same game as everyone else, and he has to play it.
2. **Every other NPC is honest, canned, and stupid.** He cannot make anyone lie for him, cover
   for him, or look the other way. If an NPC says something, it's true as far as that NPC knows.
   This rule is load-bearing — half the ways to catch him are just asking honest programs
   questions the host can't afford to have answered.
3. **The scenes run in a fixed order.** He picks *hosts*, never sets. The Computor deals the ten
   scenes (§10) like cards, in order, to both sides — nobody at the table, Oz included, chooses
   what loads next. The party always lands in the scene together.

### The tell — the ring

One thing is constant across every host: **a signet ring, a circle cut into the band that stops
just short of closing.** Twenty, not twenty-one — a loop that never quite completes.

- The party **looted it off his corpse in scene 1.** After the reboot it is gone from that PC's
  pocket. Let them discover that themselves.
- In every scene it is **on the host's hand.** He cannot take it off. Don't explain why.
- When a host dies: **black frost creeps across the lips**, the body whispers *"Twenty… not
  twenty-one,"* — and the ring is not on the body.

Never point a camera at it. It's there for the player who thinks to look at hands, and the first
time somebody does, the whole table changes how it walks into a room.

### Host death

**Oz is trying to kill you.** That is the correction to make if you take nothing else from this
section. He is not playing hide-and-seek and he is not running a haunted house — he wants the
party dead, permanently, on the record, and every scene he takes the body best placed to arrange
it.

**Dropping the host kills the host.** Not Oz. A program with a name and a job and neighbors goes
down, the frost comes, the line gets whispered, the ring is gone. The party's oldest and
best-practiced trick — kill the wizard, fast — now executes bystanders. Don't soften it and don't
moralize it; narrate the frost and move on.

The exceptions, and every one is deliberate:

- **Scenes 2 and 3: he hops.** Kill the bandit captain and another bandit straightens up wearing
  the ring; kill Big Ned and the mob grows a new ringleader mid-sentence. These two scenes exist
  to teach the rule while it's cheap — let the party kill him twice early, so they've stopped
  trying by the time it would cost them.
- **Scene 6: named, he tips his hat.** There is no fight in The Quiet Night. Call him out and
  the nobody smiles, touches his brim, and walks out of the village. That's all that happens.
- **Scene 9: killing him doesn't stick.** He's in his own body, at full strength, in his own
  library, and putting him down changes nothing. The Parley is for talking.

**Finding him does not make him leave** (scenes 2–8). Naming the host costs him the pretense —
he stops acting and starts playing the host's stat block like he's read the manual, because he
has. Exposure is not the end of the scene. It's the start of the fight, fought with whatever the
body he chose can do.

### Ending a scene

Every scene ends, and **every scene ended is spent.** The Computor will not rerun a segment that
has already played — the order marches forward and never back.

| | Route | What it takes | Pays |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Resolve it** | Complete the objective (§10). The segment finishes whether Oz was ready or not — he loses the room without being beaten, which he takes far worse than being stabbed | **+1 Trace** — the floor |
| 2 | **Break it open** | The Chief Engineer's **Panel Glitch**, or Striker's **Standard Issue** fired at the host. No objective, no fight, no negotiation — the set is ripped out from under him and the party acts first on the far side | **+1, plus a bonus box — a free pass** |

**Killing the host is not an exit.** The fire still burns, the mob still wants its hanging, the
objective still stands — the party just plays the rest of the scene without him in it (scenes 2
and 3 excepted, where he re-enters wearing someone else). Finish the job anyway; that's where the
Trace is.

> **The lesson he's teaching whether he means to or not:** twenty runs trained this party to kill
> the wizard fast, and he has built a night where killing fast just murders the man he was
> holding. Let a table work that out on their own. If they haven't by scene 4, let a dying host
> get the whole line out — *"Twenty… not twenty-one"* — while the frost spreads, and watch the
> table go quiet.

### Between scenes

The set dissolves mid-swing; everyone drops into the next scene in the order, together. HP, spell
slots, and conditions **persist** — this is one continuous fight in ten rooms.

**One breather, after scene 3-or-4.** Everything that recharges on a short rest comes back (pact
slots, Bardic Inspiration, Channel Divinity, Second Wind). **Hit points and Hit Dice do not** —
wounds only close by healing; catching your breath doesn't close one; see the doctor. It fires on
schedule regardless of how the party is doing — going Trace-less is punishment enough for a
botched objective.

**The second rest is scene 6, and it's on the board.** The Quiet Night is a **full long rest** —
hit points, Hit Dice, slots, everything. That is exactly its trap: taking it means stopping the
hunt for a whole quiet evening while Oz stands in the village square, in a nobody, doing nothing
but watching them sleep. The rest is real. So is he. Whether they dare take it *is* the scene.

### Escalation — Grudge

**The flavour runs all night; the numbers are finale-only.**

- From about scene 3, whoever he's wearing starts quoting past runs in the host's voice —
  *"You opened with the fireball in run six too."* This costs nothing, breaks no rule, and is the
  best free tell in the game. Use it every scene.
- **The numeric Grudge applies only in scenes 9–10**, in his own body. In scenes 2–8 he runs on
  the host's stock stat block, full stop — a +1 the host didn't come with would break hard
  rule 1.
- He arrives at the finale with **one Grudge per scene the party actually played** after the
  first: **+1 each** to attack rolls, saving throws, and spell save DC, scenes 9–10 only.
  **Skipped scenes pay him nothing** — a party that skips arrives sooner, healthier, *and* facing
  a weaker wizard. That's the other half of what bonus Trace buys.
- However high it runs, by scene 8 he's barely bothering to act the part. He *wants* the finale.

### Trace — a floor, not a gate

**Ten boxes.** There is **no lockout, no gate, and no cap** — nothing to fill early, nothing the
finale hides behind. Trace is a floor under the party's feet, not a wall in front of them.

| How | Trace |
|---|---|
| **Complete the scene objective** — the baseline, always available | **+1** |
| Correctly name the host *before* he strikes | **+1 bonus** |
| **Open a maintenance panel** (Chief Engineer, §5) | **+1 bonus** |
| **Standard Issue** fired at the host (First Officer, §5) | **+1 bonus** |
| Any clever use of holodeck physics the GM likes | **+1 bonus** |

**The floor:** every scene resolved pays +1, and there are ten scenes, so the track cannot stall
out. Grinding always gets there — bloodier, later, but always.

**The pass:** every **bonus** box is a **free pass** — one scene the party never has to play, and
**they choose which one.** Passes bank; there's no expiry and no limit on how many they hold.

**Spending one.** A pass is spent at the **top of a scene**: you describe what's in front of them,
and before the first roll anyone at the table can call it. The set folds, the program logs the
chapter, and they're standing in the next one. That's the whole point of making it theirs — they
get to look at the thing, decide they want no part of it tonight, and walk. Once dice are on the
table the scene is theirs to finish; a pass buys a scene skipped, not a fight abandoned.

**Passable: scenes 2–8.** Scene 1 already happened and **scenes 9 and 10 always run** — nothing
passes on the ending.

**What it costs.** The skipped scene's +1 never lands, so a pass moves the party forward without
moving the track. Say that out loud once, at the start of the night, and never again: **a pass buys
blood and time, not Trace.** They still get there — the bonus that bought the pass already paid its
own +1 — they just get there with more HP and fewer scars, facing a wizard with one less Grudge.

**Passing on The Quiet Night** costs them the long rest, and it's now their own hands doing it.
Don't warn them; don't hide it either. *"A village on an ordinary evening — nothing is wrong"* is an
honest description of that set, and if they hear "nothing is wrong" and spend a pass to skip past
it, that is a decision they made with the truth in front of them. It also leaves Oz without the
evening he uses to pick his finale target — so he picks it himself, and he picks whoever has hurt
him most.

**If the party is limping by scene 6 or 7 with no passes banked, hand them a bonus box off a good
idea** — a skipped scene is the kindest heal in the game, and arriving at the finale underpowered
is a threat to hold over the table, not a TPK to actually deliver.

### Statting Oz

**Scenes 2–8: he is the host.** Run the host's stock stat block, unmodified — a *bandit captain*,
a *thug*, a *noble*, a *commoner* with a cellar key. What makes him dangerous in the middle
scenes is not numbers; it's twenty runs of knowing exactly what this party does under pressure,
worn inside a body they haven't thought to suspect. The party's damage dies with each host —
there is nothing of *him* to wound until scene 9.

**Scenes 9–10: his own body.** Build him as a **solo CR 9-ish arcane duelist** for a level 5
party of 5, with legendary actions, the Grudge bonus (above), and one signature: he
*pre-counters* the party's habits. Practical version: give him three **Twenty Deaths**
reactions, usable once each per scene —

- **"I remember this."** — impose disadvantage on an attack that hits him.
- **"You always do that."** — negate one class feature's effect once (Sneak Attack, Smite, a Maneuver).
- **"Not this time."** — succeed on a failed save he just rolled. **This is also the answer to
  Counterspell** — the 2024 version makes *him* roll a Con save, so a solo villain would otherwise
  fold to Magister Orin's one prepared copy. Print it in his block so it lands as a bit rather
  than a rules argument at the table.

**Play him to win — in every body.** The bandit captain focuses fire, the magistrate uses the law
like a garrote, the tenor lets the party waste rounds on doors he locked himself. In the finale
he targets the weakest crew member, finishes people off, and does not pull a single punch. The
party's safety does not come from you being merciful — it comes from the fact that in scenes 2–8
he's wearing stock NPCs, and in the finale **his own hit points are finally on the table.**

**He arrives at scene 9 untouched**, no matter what the party did all night — their damage was
spent on hosts, and it died with the bodies it was dealt to. That sounds unfair and it's the
point: **Trace is the track that accumulates, not his health bar.** Say this out loud around
scene 3, when someone notices, or they'll think they're making progress in the wrong currency.

**Sizing the finale:** give him enough HP that a focused level 5 party of five needs about
**three rounds** to drop him — roughly 100, adjusted for your table's nova. Fewer players, less
HP. He should feel like a wall — the first one all night that stays hit.

## 9. Safeties off — what "real" means

- Damage from anything in the program is real. **0 HP = death saves.**
- Death is real and **nothing in this game undoes it.** No resurrection, no Revivify, no crash
  cart. What keeps the table full is the **Redshirt engine** — and you should extend it to
  *anyone* who dies, not just the Redshirt. A crewman wanders in looking for the gym, the doors
  slam, and that player is back on a fresh pregen. Nobody watches from the couch.
- **Healing works — yours and his.** Don't explain how (§6). Death is the thing that's permanent.
- Budget the real damage: **~10–12 average HP per PC per scene**, ~8 in scenes 2–4. That's the
  number that makes ten persistent scenes survivable (§6).
- Environmental hazards are the actual threat: the flood, the collapse, the siege engines.
- The arch is locked. The Computor answers, but only in the most literal, unhelpful way, and it
  is *very* proud of how well it fulfilled the request.

## 10. The ten scenes

Each is one Foundry scene. Roughly 15–25 minutes each. **They run in this order, every time.**
Oz never chooses the set — he only chooses who to wear when it loads. The first room and the last
room are the same room.

| # | Scene | The set | Oz's host | Objective (+1 Trace) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Run Twenty** | Cold open. The trophy room — his sanctum, his corpse, confetti. Then the reboot | *himself* — and then he sits up | Survive the reboot; learn the safeties are off. Somebody loots the ring |
| 2 | **The Road to Town** | A bandit ambush on the road | the bandit captain | Get the whole party through the ambush alive. Kill the captain and he hops to another bandit — watch the hands |
| 3 | **The Market Square** | An open-air lynching — a mob, a rope, a grievance | **Big Ned**, a dead bandit's brother | Stop the hanging. He hops here too, once — it's the last free lesson |
| 4 | **The Short Drop** | A lawful hanging — scaffold, paperwork, a drum | the magistrate | Get the condemned off the scaffold. The law is honest even when the judge isn't — use it |
| 5 | **Last Orders** | The inn. Hired mercenaries at every table, paid in honest coin | the innkeeper | Get out of the inn alive. The mercs were honestly hired, and the contract is real and readable |
| 6 | **The Quiet Night** | A village on an ordinary evening. Nothing is wrong | a nobody, who does nothing | Rest — a full long rest, and that's the trap (§8). Name him and he tips his hat and leaves |
| 7 | **The Fire** | An opera house, mid-performance. He locked the doors | the lead tenor | Get everyone out of the building |
| 8 | **The Guide** | Catacombs. Someone has to lead the way down, and he made sure it's him | the hired guide | Reach the far side — without going where he steers you |
| 9 | **The Parley** | His library. Quiet, warm, two chairs | *himself* — **unkillable.** Dropping him at full strength doesn't stick | Hear him out, or don't. Leave knowing something you can use |
| 10 | **Twenty-One** | The trophy room again — wrong-colored, no exits, and **no NPCs** | *himself* — **killable.** There is nobody left to wear | Kill him. It sticks this time |

**Damage budget:** ~10–12 average real HP per PC per scene, ~8 in scenes 2–4. the Doctor heals each
crew member once per scene (2d8+5, ~14), so that budget is deliberately just under what he can
undo — the party bleeds slowly all night. The **breather** fires after scene 3-or-4, and **scene 6
is the long rest**, if they dare take it (§8); mark both on your scene list before you start.

**The pass rule.** Every bonus Trace box banks a **free pass**, and the party picks which scene
(2–8) it burns — spent at the top of a scene, after your description and before the first roll
(§8). Don't run sets just because they're written. The party reaching the Parley after seven
scenes instead of nine is them *winning*, not you losing prep, and the passed sets were never his
to keep anyway. Scenes 9 and 10 always run; nothing passes on the ending. If the party is limping
with no passes banked, see the mercy note under Trace (§8) and lighten their road.

Keep a visible count — index cards, poker chips, anything. A pass they forget they have is a pass
that does nothing, and the whole mechanic only pays off if they're weighing it at the door of
every scene.

**Ending beat:** Oz dies, the arch unlocks, the Computor chirps *"End program. Difficulty
feedback: was this challenge satisfactory?"* Let the Captain answer.

## 12. Open questions for Joe

1. **Player count** — how many seats? Under 5, cut roles (keep Captain, Engineer, Counselor,
   Security, Redshirt — those five carry the mechanics).
2. **Is the Redshirt a guaranteed seat or optional?** It's the best bit; consider making it a
   mandatory role someone must take.
3. **Does the pass economy pace the night right?** — tune after a dry run. Ten scenes at +1 is
   the floor; a sharp table should burn two or three passes and hit the Parley early and healthy.
   If a dry run passes on half the night, make the bonus sources rarer, not smaller — the passes
   are the fun part. Watch for hoarding, too: a table that banks four and spends none has turned
   the reward into homework, and the fix is to remind them at a door, not to add a rule.
4. **Do you want Oz statted in `combat-runner`** (`#combat-runner` NPC + `actions.jsonl` rows)
   so the GUI can run him live, or is he Foundry-only?
5. **Pregen sheets** — build in Foundry from compendium, or author as `.md` first?
6. **One heal per crew member per scene** — that's ~14 HP each against a ~10–12 HP scene budget,
   so the party bleeds slowly. If a dry run runs hot, the only knob to turn is letting him heal
   the same person twice in a scene. Don't add rules; turn that one dial.
